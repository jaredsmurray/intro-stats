# Teach-back and transfer probe design

Use probes to catch smooth-reading misconceptions that do not produce a stall.
Derive them from the chapter objectives before reviewing agent output. Keep the
answer key with the technical auditor or structure editor, not the pedagogical
reader.

Use the smallest useful set. A Standard review normally needs one or two probes
per central objective, not a shadow problem set.

## Probe types

### Explain

Ask the reader to state the concept in plain language and identify what varies,
what is fixed, and what the quantity measures.

Example shape: “What does a standard error describe, and what would make it
smaller?”

### Distinguish

Place nearby concepts side by side and ask what differs. Target confusions the
chapter must prevent, not every advanced nuance.

Example shape: “How is a prediction interval different from a confidence
interval in what it covers?”

### Interpret

Give a table, formula, or figure from the chapter and ask what conclusion it
supports—and what it does not support.

Example shape: “What does the lower risk of this portfolio allow us to say
about its component assets?”

### Transfer

Change the surface details while preserving the underlying structure. Ask the
reader to predict, choose, or explain.

Example shape: “If the sample size were four times as large, how would the
standard error change?”

### Reconstruct

Ask the reader to reproduce one central arithmetic or logical step using only
visible inputs.

Example shape: “Use the displayed mean and standard error to reconstruct the
interval.”

### Boundary

Ask for the condition under which a rule stops applying. Use this when
overgeneralization is a serious risk.

Example shape: “When does correlation fail to summarize the relationship in a
scatterplot?”

### Literal instantiation

Apply a central verbal gloss to the exact support, values, or geometry of its
worked example. Ask what the words predict the reader should observe, then
check whether that observation is possible.

Example shape: “Apply the chapter's interpretation of this spread measure to
each possible outcome in the displayed two-outcome distribution. What does the
reported value mean here?”

## Scoring

Do not score for polished wording. Record:

- `supported`: the chapter supplies the needed rule and the reader applies it;
- `fragile`: the right answer depends on an unstated inference or lucky recall;
- `misconception`: the chapter supports a plausible wrong rule;
- `not taught`: the probe exceeds the objective or prior boundary.

For `fragile` and `misconception`, cite the exact passage or display that
produced the answer. A failed probe is evidence only when the expected answer
is actually within the chapter's scope.
